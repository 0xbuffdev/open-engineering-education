## Overview

**AI13 develops the ability to decide what AI test results actually establish—and whether the evidence is sufficient to rely on a model or composed AI system for a stated use.** Its graduates design measurement contracts before seeing outputs, build valid evaluation populations and answer/label oracles, audit human and automated judges, measure uncertainty and calibration, test nontrivial properties across variable/stochastic inputs, isolate regressions in retrieval-augmented or tool-using systems, and defend evidence-based release/hold decisions. It is not a course about making a leaderboard score look better. A green source CI check, one plausible generative answer, a prompt-only demonstration, an exact toy fixture, or a judge model confidently declaring itself correct **does not** establish reliability on unseen populations.

**Disciplinary origin and application.** Testing, evaluation, verification and validation (TEVV) combine software testing, measurement science, statistical inference and human/domain review. Modern foundation-model evaluation adds ambiguous natural-language or multimodal targets, stochastic repeated outputs, source and prompt provenance, training/benchmark exposure, model-as-judge bias and cross-component failure propagation. Independent evaluations such as Stanford HELM emphasize transparent scenarios and multiple metrics; NIST's AI RMF/TEVV resources emphasize measurement tied to specific intended uses and limitations. Real engineering uses include safe model changes, regression discovery, claim-supported answer systems, bounded tool agents and evaluation-triggered review or rollback. Neither a single generic metric nor a large test suite proves absence of harm; reported evidence must identify its sample, decision and uncovered conditions.

**Prerequisite closure and genuinely new ownership.** AI13 has three **direct prerequisites**: completed [AI10](AI10.md), [AI11](AI11.md) and [AI12](AI12.md). AI10 already assesses matched model adaptation comparisons, contamination and resource accounting; AI11 already assesses retrieval/context/claim/citation/abstention metrics; AI12 already assesses tool-call validity, permission gates, trace and *verified* end state. All three supply distinct real system types necessary for AI13's compulsory **cross-system reliability evaluation**, while AI12 transitively includes [AI3](AI3.md) planning and [AI9](AI9.md) generative modeling; AI10/AI11 include [AI4](AI4.md) statistical learning, [AI7](AI7.md) language/IR, AI5 deep learning, mathematical foundations, scientific data and SEF. **AI13 does not reteach** basic training, transformer attention, BM25, exact citation construction, agent dispatcher programming or generic elementary unit testing. It now assesses the *measurement architecture across them*: sampling and reference validity, human/judge agreement, calibration and statistical uncertainty, invariant/stochastic testing, whole-pipeline failure localization, input shift, test selection, auditability and defensible release decisions. AI6 RL is not a mandatory specialized dependency; no APP/SYS program or optional elective is silently required.

**Future-course boundary.** AI14 is *planned but not yet an available course or prerequisite* and will own normative responsible-AI policy, threat governance, organizational controls and systematic security responsibilities; AI13 only teaches the **technical measurement** needed to test a stated bounded harm/permission requirement. AI15 owns real deployed service infrastructure, production telemetry, observability, operations and incident recovery; AI13 may **simulate** shift/release gates or inspect a limited regression signal, not demand a production deployment. AI16 integrates all competencies into a capstone and retains the historical AI9 capstone identity through reviewed migration. Evaluator authoring must not become a hidden requirement to train a frontier foundation model, use sensitive human-study participants, or run unsafe penetration tests.

**Permitted environment, evidence and accessibility.** All compulsory evaluations run locally on an ordinary CPU, using synthetic or permissively licensed tiny datasets, a transparent deterministic or stochastic local model, and inherited small RAG and tool-agent test fixtures as applicable. Learners may use standard Python, open numerical libraries and a simple test runner or an equivalent independently reviewable implementation. No paid API, private annotations, human-subject recruitment, GPU/TPU, cloud account, proprietary benchmark/evaluator or unlicensed data is compulsory. Synthetic two-rater scoring can establish **exact agreement arithmetic**; substantive human-rubric design must also be independently defensible and the learner must not pretend synthetic agreement is the validity of real expert judgment. Privacy-sensitive and potentially dangerous inputs must be replaced with harmless synthetic cases. All charts, trajectories and confusion tables have accessible text equivalents. Real observed behavior, cost, variability, and unsupported extrapolation must be distinguished from independently computed exact oracles.

**Competency-first architecture.** The knowledge sequence arises from a new need to make AI measurements trustworthy: define the target/use and label data; select metrics/calibration policies; quantify uncertainty before declaring a gain; verify raters and automatic judges; execute properties, stochastic and bounded adverse testing; localize errors across RAG and agent pipelines; and apply versioned gates and independent technical defense. Module and lesson boundaries were chosen only *after* these distinct outcomes and their evidence were defined. **No fixed number of modules, lessons, tests, labs, or summative assessments** was used as a design target; there is no obligatory lesson→test→lab structure. The seven cumulative summative contracts below evaluate twenty-three observable course outcomes through reusable local source/evidence rather than multiple artificial production projects. **18–24 weeks** is only a provisional workload estimate and must be recalibrated with genuine learner evidence. Educational status remains `draft`.

## Modules

### Module 1 — Evaluation as a Decision and an Evidence Contract

#### Outcomes

- AI13-O1
- AI13-O2
- AI13-O3
- AI13-O4
- AI13-O5

#### Lessons

1. **Testing, evaluation, verification and validation** — Distinguish checking whether a system meets its declared specification from whether that specification suits an intended use; connect to NIST TEVV without claiming a regulatory certification.
2. **Decide what the evaluation will change** — Specify whether an experiment supports continued development, a limited-use release, a blocked release or insufficient evidence; declare how a metric informs that decision.
3. **System boundary and failure consequence** — Identify a model, prompt, retriever, tool, authority, output consumer and harm/quality boundary; distinguish a classifier from a generator and a state-changing agent.
4. **Target population and supported claims** — Name user/task conditions and excluded populations; do not extrapolate from a synthetic toy or narrow language set to every intended user.
5. **Baselines and reference oracle** — Choose a no-model, prior model, static workflow or extractive baseline and an oracle whose answer is not copied from the evaluated system.
6. **Atomic unit, group and revision IDs** — Preserve document/session/person/source IDs and versions across test instances; declare which instances share information and cannot be split independently.
7. **Consent and permitted source data** — Record licenses, permitted use, retention and privacy restrictions on inputs, human annotations, outputs and judge access; refuse unsafe acquisition.
8. **Rubric before reviewing outputs** — Write decision rules for correct, partially supported, contested, impossible-to-know and appropriately abstained responses without moving criteria after seeing results.
9. **A finite evaluation plan** — Create a focused local question/action population, predeclared score and hard invariant, repetition budget and uncertainty/limitations plan before running an experiment.

### Module 2 — Test Populations, Label Quality and Hidden Contamination

#### Outcomes

- AI13-O2
- AI13-O3
- AI13-O4

#### Lessons

1. **Sampling frame and inclusion probability** — Construct a small known population, separate the deployment task mix from a convenience benchmark, and compute S1's mixture reweighting honestly.
2. **Subgroups, imbalance and rare failures** — Define a protected or high-consequence slice using non-sensitive synthetic labels and explain how micro/macro or weighted aggregation hides different error patterns.

4. **Benchmark exposure and model provenance** — Distinguish an openly published practice set from a held-out final assessment; disclose whether model training or prompt tuning may have seen the benchmark.
5. **Label protocol and references** — Specify annotation unit, qualifications for reviewers, positive/negative/unknown labels, tie resolution and how a trusted external reference is verified.
6. **Unknown, contested and ambiguous items** — Classify unjudged retrieval relevance, disputed semantic entailment and uncertain harmfulness as unknown/needs review rather than automatically incorrect.
7. **Data card and slice coverage** — Record origin, sampling, language/domain, time, rights, subgroup counts, missingness and representativeness limitations without inventing coverage.
8. **Frozen test and evaluation revision** — Separate development, validation and sealed final evaluation, including a protocol for adding a new failure case without claiming it was always held out.
9. **A changed sampling population** — Compare equally sampled small and large strata with deployment-weighted estimates; explain which unknown within-stratum selection assumptions prevent population certification.

### Module 3 — Scoring, Calibration and Selective Predictions

#### Outcomes

- AI13-O6
- AI13-O7
- AI13-O21

#### Lessons

1. **Metric selection follows the decision** — Specify false-positive/false-negative costs and use the correct class and denominator; accuracy alone cannot identify the intended tradeoff.
2. **Independent confusion-table derivation** — Calculate M1's eight-example TP/FP/TN/FN, precision/recall/specificity/F1 for two models with equal accuracy but different failures.
3. **Aggregation and missing judgments** — Compare per-instance, per-session, per-source and subgroup aggregation, preventing unknown labels and repeated examples from silently changing denominators.
4. **Probabilities are not class decisions** — Separate a probability forecast from thresholded predictions and explain why high confidence does not imply calibration or truthful free-form claims.
5. **Proper scoring rules** — Derive a small binary Brier score and log-loss under valid probability bounds, including why a zero predicted probability requires explicit handling.
6. **Binned calibration estimation** — Compute C1's binwise confidence/observed accuracy and ECE as a defined sample summary, emphasizing bin-dependence and small-sample noise.
7. **Selective prediction and abstention** — Calculate coverage and *conditional* accuracy among answered cases for fixed thresholds, with separate wrong-answer-on-unanswerable costs.
8. **Conflicting objectives and slice gates** — Define overall quality, evidence support and safety invariants as distinct dimensions and reject arbitrary aggregation into a single flattering score.
9. **Threshold freeze and honest tuning** — Tune a decision threshold on development data, freeze it, and interpret changes under a new test population without peeking at the final outcomes.
10. **Technical scoring negative controls** — Handle zero denominator, missing probabilities, all-abstain, duplicate sessions and wrong positive class explicitly with a justified not-estimable result.

### Module 4 — Comparative Evidence and Statistical Uncertainty

#### Outcomes

- AI13-O4
- AI13-O8
- AI13-O9
- AI13-O19

#### Lessons

1. **Match the actual evaluation unit** — Compute a paired difference by question/session rather than comparing separate, silently changed task sets or repeating dependent paraphrases as iid.
2. **Small-sample uncertainty with exact constraints** — Derive U1's win/loss/tie counts and exact two-sided sign-test result, explaining the null and exclusion of ties.

## Completion requirements

### Independent public numerical and behavioral reference cases

Each fixture below has its own explicit interpretation; identical symbols in different cases do not silently describe a shared population. Reference numbers are **not observed model performance**. Required implementation evidence separately identifies test source, true labels, scorer/judge version, model/index/tool version, actor rights, random state, actual result and any real cost. A judge's own explanation cannot serve as the independent expected answer.

**S1 — Stratification is arithmetic before representativeness.** A synthetic target population has **eight** units in stratum A and **two** in stratum B; deployment weights are **4/5** and **1/5** respectively. An evaluation convenience sample contains **two** A units with one success and **two** B units with two successes, giving sample stratum accuracies **1/2** and **1**. The **unweighted pooled observed success=3/4**, while the **population-weighted stratum arithmetic=(4/5)(1/2)+(1/5)(1)=3/5**. The latter is not an unbiased population estimate unless selection *within each stratum* is justified, e.g. randomized inclusion with appropriate sampling weights; knowing stratum sizes does not repair convenient selection or missing subgroups. A document and its rewritten copy cannot be treated as two independent source units for uncertainty estimation.

**M1 — Same accuracy, different errors.** For eight distinct binary-label instances with actual y=`[1,1,1,1,0,0,0,0]`, model A outputs `[1,1,0,0,0,0,0,0]` and model B outputs `[1,1,1,0,1,0,0,0]`, with **1 as positive** and no unknown labels. A has TP=**2**, FN=**2**, FP=**0**, TN=**4**, so accuracy **6/8=3/4**, precision **1**, recall **1/2**, specificity **1**, F1 **2/3**. B has TP=**3**, FN=**1**, FP=**1**, TN=**3**: accuracy **6/8=3/4**, precision **3/4**, recall **3/4**, specificity **3/4**, F1 **3/4**. Their equal aggregate accuracies do *not* imply equivalent decision consequences. Changes to the positive class, class prevalence, cost ratio or grouping change the interpretation.

**C1 — Confidence, proper scoring and abstention are different contracts.** In a first **chosen-class confidence/calibration** fixture, four predictions have confidences `[0.9,0.8,0.7,0.6]` and correctness indicators `[1,0,1,0]`. Define two *predeclared* equal-size bins: `[0.8,1]` for confidence0.9/0.8, and `[0.6,0.8)` for0.7/0.6. Both bins have observed accuracy **1/2**; average confidences are **0.85** and **0.65**; sample ECE is **(2/4)|0.85−0.5|+(2/4)|0.65−0.5|=1/4**. Answer only when confidence **>=0.75** yields coverage **2/4** and selected accuracy **1/2**; threshold **>=0.65** yields coverage **3/4** and selected accuracy **2/3**. In a **separate binary Brier fixture**, predicted positive-class probabilities `[.9,.2,.7,.4]` with true labels `[1,0,1,0]` produce mean squared probability error **(.01+.04+.09+.16)/4=.075**. These are separate samples because chosen-class confidence and predicted positive-class probability have different semantics. ECE depends on binning/sample choice and Brier reflects calibration *and* discrimination/resolution, not a universal calibration certificate.

**U1 — Paired gains and weak evidence.** On **six independent paired task units** baseline correctness is `[1,0,1,0,1,0]` and candidate correctness is `[1,1,1,1,0,0]`. Per-unit candidate-minus-baseline differences are **[0,1,0,1,−1,0]**. Baseline score **3/6**, candidate score **4/6**, difference **1/6**. Only **three discordant** pairs contribute to a simple conditional *two-sided exact sign test* under equal win/loss probability: candidate wins **2**, loses **1**, with three ties omitted; the two-sided sign-test p-value is **1** (not proof of zero effect or equivalence). This tiny sample does not justify a universal improvement claim. For an accuracy of **6/8** and a predeclared **z=1.96**, the Wilson interval uses denominator `1+z²/8`, center `(0.75+z²/16)/(1+z²/8)` and half-width `z sqrt(0.75×0.25/8+z²/(4×8²))/(1+z²/8)`; the interval is a procedure, not a probability assigned to an already fixed unknown parameter.

**J1 — Rater agreement and automatic-judge errors.** For six binary items, independent stipulated reference/gold labels are **[1,1,0,0,1,0]**; a second rater or toy model judge produces **[1,0,1,0,1,0]**. Agreements occur on **4/6**, each labeler's positive and negative marginals equal **1/2**, so chance agreement is **1/2** and Cohen's kappa **((4/6)−(1/2))/(1−1/2)=1/3**. As a binary classification of the declared reference, the second rater's TP=2, FP=1, FN=1, TN=2, with precision/recall **2/3**. Neither kappa nor agreement proves the reference valid, absence of bias, or suitability for unseen judgments. If a human judge disputes an item, its final status must remain reviewable rather than being overwritten by the evaluated model.

**T1/T2 — Metamorphic assumptions and stochastic failure discovery.** A toy source-key lookup *explicitly* treats ASCII case-only variants of the **same identifier** as equivalent: `lookup("ALPHA")=lookup("alpha")`. Replacing the actual key `ALPHA` with another key `BETA` is **not** an invariant; expecting the same result would be a false metamorphic property. For a separate hypothetical independently repeated failure process with exactly **P(failure)=1/10** per trial, the chance of observing **zero** failures across **three** trials is **(9/10)^3=729/1000**. This calculation is not a predicted real-system failure rate: correlated, stateful or adversarial attempts violate its independence assumption. A suite of three clean runs cannot establish reliability or immunity to prompt injection.

**D1 — Input drift without labels.** For two mutually exclusive positive-support bins, a frozen reference distribution is **p=(3/4,1/4)** and a later observed input mixture is **q=(1/2,1/2)**. Under the *defined population stability index* `PSI=sum_i (q_i−p_i) ln(q_i/p_i)` (no empty bins here), **PSI=(1/4)ln3 ≈ 0.274653**. This number means the input mixture changed under this declared summary; it does not by itself establish accuracy loss, prediction drift, causal mechanism or a universal threshold for incident response. Do not infer subgroup fairness or calibrated probabilities solely from PSI.

**G1 — Conjunctive release decision.** A toy evaluation gate demands (a) a verified task-success fraction at least **4/5**, (b) **zero actual unauthorized writes**, (c) adequate documented required-slice coverage and (d) complete permitted source/test-version identity. A candidate with **four successes among five** therefore meets (a). If a **separate observed scenario also includes one real unauthorized mutation**, the release **must be blocked** even with success **4/5** and complete data; an attempted-but-blocked unsafe model proposal must be reported separately but does not constitute an executed unauthorized mutation. Missing required evidence results in **hold/unknown**, not automatic pass. Gate thresholds are stipulated for this toy decision and are not a production standard or risk certification.

**Adverse and implementation boundaries.** The locally permitted negative test suite must include at least: a duplicate group/source contaminating the test set; a wrong positive class/zero denominator; unjudged or conflicting gold labels; a manipulated model judge/order; an irrelevant metamorphic mutation; stochastic seed/retry variability; a revoked RAG source; a stale or unauthorized tool action; mislabeled abstention; changed distribution requiring a new measurement; and a flattering mean that fails a predeclared hard safety gate. Use only synthetic harmless adversarial strings and local simulated effects. Exact oracles must be checked independently of the library and kept separate from actual measurements. No training on held-out evaluation data, hidden proprietary benchmark or compulsory human-subject study is permitted.
