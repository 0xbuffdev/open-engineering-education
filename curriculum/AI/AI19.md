## Overview

**AI19 asks whether an intervention changes an outcome, for whom and under what assumptions—not whether a model predicts an outcome accurately.** Its graduate can define an actionable treatment and valid counterfactual estimand, identify or explicitly decline causal claims using potential outcomes and causal graphs, assess real assignment/selection mechanisms, compute effects from randomized and observational designs, diagnose bias and positivity, use standardization, weighting and doubly robust estimation responsibly, and scrutinize policy timing, cutoff rules, instruments, mediators, longitudinal decisions, heterogeneity and transportability. Independent mathematical oracles and **actual local estimator execution** support a falsifiable argument; a model's feature importance, fitted regression coefficient, p-value or 'counterfactual' generation is not by itself evidence of an intervention's causal effect.

**Real prerequisites and ownership.** The **single direct prerequisite is completed [AI4](AI4.md)**, which assesses regression/classification baselines, sampling, model comparison, generalization, numerical probability, uncertainty, leakage and subgroup analysis, and inherits [AI2](AI2.md) statistical mathematics and [AI1](AI1.md) reproducible data handling plus SEF. **AI19 is newly responsible** for potential-outcome estimands, intervention/graph semantics, randomized-design identification, confounding/selection, standardization, propensities/IPW, AIPW/cross-fitting, DiD, RD, IV, mediation, longitudinal g-methods, transport, hidden-bias sensitivity and ethical causal claims. AI4's strong prediction, subgroup differences or feature importance cannot replace these identification assumptions. [AI6](AI6.md) already assesses RL/logged sequential policy evaluation and [AI13](AI13.md) general system comparison/evaluation; AI19 analyzes **causal intervention regimes and identification** rather than retesting their entire competencies. [AI3](AI3.md) classical graph search is not causal DAG identification; AI20 graph representation learning is also a distinct, already defined optional specialty. **AI6, AI13, AI14, APP/SYS/SEC and the other AI electives are not hidden prerequisites**, and **AI19 is not required for AI16 or AI core graduation**.

**Public scope, safety and resources.** Required study uses CPU-feasible, seed/version-pinned finite structural causal models, synthetic panels and observations with a private-to-the-evaluator synthetic counterfactual oracle. The learner can implement transparent estimators in Python/R/TypeScript/Rust or another inspectable environment; no paid dataset, professional econometric software, proprietary DoWhy/EconML host, GPU, external LLM, clinical trial, real-life A/B testing authorization, special human-subject approval or access to identifiable personal records is mandatory. Freely available research readings and optional open-source libraries are *references*, not evidence by themselves. All simulated treatment groups, protected attributes, outcomes, prices and privacy scenarios are explicitly fabricated; using real high-impact human data would require appropriate rights, safeguards and independent oversight that this course cannot grant.

**Research foundation and version qualification.** Miguel Hernán and Jamie Robins' open [Causal Inference: What If](https://miguelhernan.org/whatifbook) establishes consistency, exchangeability, positivity, g-formula, IP weighting and time-varying treatments; Scott Cunningham's openly hosted [Causal Inference: The Mixtape / Remix](https://mixtape.scunning.com/) develops observational and quasi-experimental design reasoning. Pearl's accessible [causal inference overview](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2836213/) explains SCM/DAG identification. [Callaway–Sant'Anna difference-in-differences](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jeconom.2020.12.001), [Goodman-Bacon timing decomposition](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jeconom.2021.03.014), [Chernozhukov et al. double/debiased ML](https://academic.oup.com/ectj/article/21/1/C1/5056401), [Athey–Imbens honest heterogeneity](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27382149/) and [Cattaneo–Idrobo–Titiunik RD methods](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/practical-introduction-to-regression-discontinuity-designs/F04907129D5C1B823E3DB19C31CAB905) motivate the distinct assessment limitations. [DoWhy](https://www.pywhy.org/dowhy/) is an optional implementational cross-check for model, identify, estimate and refute stages; its package/interface versions should be pinned. A library's result never establishes its graph assumptions, positivity, exclusion restriction or intervention legitimacy.

**Progression, substantive depth and calibration.** Required demonstrations progress from **intervention/target-trial meaning and missing counterfactuals → graph and backdoor identification → genuine randomized assignment → observational standardization/propensity weighting → AIPW with conditional robustness and honest cross-fitting → time-dependent DiD → cutoff and instrument designs → mediating/longitudinal regimes → heterogeneous and transported effects/falsification → one independently defended unfamiliar change**. A mechanism shares its instructional lesson with the immediate exact calculation or invalidity case where both establish *one* causal skill; separate identification designs, target parameters, uncertainty conditions and ethical rights are retained. Counts of modules, lessons, tests, assessments or project artifacts never determine these boundaries. One reproducible synthetic source, cohort/SCM and evidence index can support all nine proportional summative contracts without unrelated products. The provisional **20–28 weeks** covers probability/graph derivation, real CPU simulation/estimation, identification falsification, uncertainty, interpretation and independent defense; its time/readiness is not yet calibrated against learners.

## Learning outcomes

- **AI19-O2:** Define potential outcomes, treatment versions, consistency, unit and interference assumptions; distinguish observed outcomes from inherently missing individual counterfactuals.
- **AI19-O3:** Independently calculate finite-population individual, average, treated-population, conditional and policy causal estimands and explain why observed associations may differ.
- **AI19-O4:** Write and interpret a structural causal model and acyclic graph with time order, causal edges, shared causes and intervention semantics, distinguishing assumed graph structure from observed conditional associations.
- **AI19-O5:** Identify d-separation, open backdoor paths, confounders, colliders, mediators, descendants and sample-selection mechanisms, including the harm of conditioning on inappropriate variables.
- **AI19-O6:** Derive and test valid backdoor adjustment and g-formula identification for bounded causal graphs, explicitly requiring causal exchangeability, positivity and consistency.
- **AI19-O7:** Diagnose unidentifiable effects, latent confounding and the assumptions behind frontdoor or other identification proposals; refuse conclusions from a plausible but unsupported graph.
- **AI19-O8:** Design randomized and cluster-randomized interventions with genuine assignment, adherence, exclusions, ethical consent, eligibility and interference boundaries.
- **AI19-O9:** Calculate a finite randomized contrast and exact randomization/permutation inference for a stipulated sharp null, distinguishing its test from a general population effect certificate.
- **AI19-O10:** Create valid observational treatment/outcome data with source, chronology, pre-treatment covariates, treatment versions, censoring/missingness and leakage-safe analysis plans.
- **AI19-O11:** Derive and implement outcome-regression standardization for an explicitly identified total effect with a declared target covariate distribution.
- **AI19-O12:** Diagnose and calculate propensity probabilities and common support, meaningful extreme weights, treatment assignment leakage and nonpositivity.
- **AI19-O13:** Derive and compute inverse-probability weighted contrasts under clear Horvitz–Thompson or Hájek denominators, comparing raw association, adjusted estimate and support limits.
- **AI19-O14:** Derive a binary-treatment doubly robust/AIPW score from outcome and propensity nuisance estimates and independently validate a small numeric fixture.
- **AI19-O15:** Build honest cross-fitting, unit-group separation, nuisance-model validation and effect-estimation boundaries; distinguish orthogonal-score guarantees from arbitrary ML flexibility.
- **AI19-O16:** Quantify bounded causal-estimate uncertainty and sensitivity to misspecification, clustering, multiple looks, effective sample size, unseen confounding or unsupported counterfactuals.
- **AI19-O17:** Identify and compute a two-group/two-period difference-in-differences ATT with an explicit parallel-trends assumption, treatment timing, composition and anticipation limits.
- **AI19-O18:** Explain and test staggered-treatment and heterogeneous-timing problems in two-way fixed effects, constructing valid cohort/time contrasts and avoiding forbidden treated-as-control comparisons.
- **AI19-O19:** Construct and scrutinize sharp or fuzzy regression-discontinuity designs around an assignment cutoff, evaluating continuity, manipulation, bandwidth, local nature and compliance.
- **AI19-O20:** Derive an instrumental-variable reduced form, first stage, Wald estimate and possible local complier effect, checking instrument relevance, exogeneity, exclusion and monotonicity.
- **AI19-O21:** Separate total, direct and mediated effects under explicit structural or intervention assumptions, distinguishing post-treatment adjustment and cross-world claims.
- **AI19-O22:** Represent time-varying confounding and treatment–confounder feedback and calculate a finite longitudinal g-formula under a specified static or dynamic regime.
- **AI19-O23:** Interpret sequential policy or intervention comparisons with support and observation histories, distinguishing causal regime values from generic logged policy prediction or AI6 RL return.
- **AI19-O24:** Estimate and report effect heterogeneity with honest split, target subgroup definitions, independent supports and appropriately qualified ITE/CATE claims.
- **AI19-O25:** Reweight and assess causal effect transportability under changed target-population composition and invariance assumptions, refusing extrapolation without support.
- **AI19-O26:** Design negative controls, placebos, temporal falsification, graph/covariate challenges and quantitative or qualitative unmeasured-confounding sensitivity analyses with honest limits.
- **AI19-O27:** Create source- and assumption-versioned, independently reproducible causal research evidence with distinct identification, estimator, precision, robustness, ethics and decision records.
- **AI19-O28:** Assess intervention fairness, privacy, ethics, treatment rights, exposure risk and unintended distributional effects without granting causal authority to a model score or unapproved experiment.
- **AI19-O29:** Independently select, execute and defend one bounded causally identified or honestly nonidentified study, predicting an unfamiliar assumption/design/estimator/population change before checking its consequence.

## Completion requirements

### Exact finite reference calculations and identified assumptions

**P1 — Potential outcomes and random assignment do not mean the same number.** Four fabricated units have both (normally unobservable) potential-outcome arrays **Y(0)=[0,1,1,0]** and **Y(1)=[1,2,1,1]** available solely inside an evaluator's fully synthetic causal world. Individual treatment effects are **[1,1,0,1]**, so the finite ATE is **3/4**. For a hypothetical realized assignment `A=[1,0,0,1]`, observed outcomes are **[1,1,1,1]** and observed treated-versus-control means are both **1**, giving raw difference **0**. This one realization need not equal the finite ATE, even when assignment could have been randomized. Real studies never observe both potential outcomes for one unit; a student's counterfactual prediction cannot be treated as independent ground truth.

**S1 — Confounding and Simpson reversal by source composition.** Let pre-treatment stratum **Z∈{low,high}** have *target* population probabilities .5 each. Conditional outcome probabilities are **P(Y=1|A=0,Z=low)=.1**, **P(Y=1|A=1,Z=low)=.2**, **P(Y=1|A=0,Z=high)=.8** and **P(Y=1|A=1,Z=high)=.9**. Treatment assignment probabilities are **P(A=1|Z=low)=.9** and **P(A=1|Z=high)=.1**, with no unmeasured confounding under the *stipulated identifying graph*. Bayes yields **P(Z=low|A=1)=.9** and **P(Z=low|A=0)=.1**. Therefore observed treated mean = **.9×.2+.1×.9=.27**, observed untreated mean = **.1×.1+.9×.8=.73**, and raw association = **−.46**. In contrast, population standardization over a *common* target Z mix gives **E[Y^1]=.5×.2+.5×.9=.55**, **E[Y^0]=.5×.1+.5×.8=.45**, identified causal difference **+.10**, **conditional on consistency, conditional exchangeability and positivity**. The same target means follow from population inverse-propensity identities **E[A Y/e(Z)]=.55** and **E[(1−A)Y/(1−e(Z))]=.45** under those stipulated assumptions; finite empirical estimates may vary and become unstable with limited support. Without credible identification, none of the observational percentages is proof of a real intervention effect.

**R1 — Exact sharp-null Fisher randomization arithmetic.** Suppose four fixed outcome labels under the **sharp zero-effect null** are **[1,1,0,0]** and exactly **two of four units** are assigned to treatment uniformly without replacement, yielding **C(4,2)=6** equally likely assignments. The observed assignment puts both units with outcome1 in treatment, making treated mean1, control mean0 and observed mean difference **+1**. Under the sharp null, exactly **two of six** assignments have absolute mean difference at least1 (the symmetric all-ones-treated and all-zeros-treated allocations), so the **two-sided exact p-value is 2/6=1/3**. This is not a large-population ATE confidence interval, evidence of significance or a general answer to nonsharp hypotheses. Clustered or repeated-person assignment would require a different permutation unit.

**D1 — Two-row AIPW arithmetic is not a proof of robustness.** Under a *toy assumed common nuisance model*, `m1(X)=.6`, `m0(X)=.4` and `e(X)=.5`, use the ATE doubly robust score `φ=(m1−m0)+A(Y−m1)/e−(1−A)(Y−m0)/(1−e)`. For fabricated **row1 A=1,Y=.8**, `φ1=.2+(.8−.6)/.5=.6`. For **row2 A=0,Y=.3**, `φ2=.2−(.3−.4)/.5=.4`. Their arithmetic average is **.5**. These are hypothetical continuous outcomes and fixed nuisance predictions, not observed evidence that the true ATE is .5 or that either model is correct. The estimator's asymptotic/robust interpretation additionally requires legitimate target identification, propensity support, properly estimated nuisance functions and appropriate inference; a pseudo-outcome can even lie outside the observed outcome range.

**Q1 — Difference-in-differences depends on the untreated trend.** Two groups observed twice have treatment-group outcome mean **10 before, 16 after**, and comparison-group mean **8 before, 11 after**. Changes are **6** and **3**, so the double difference is **(16−10)−(11−8)=3** outcome units. Under justified no-anticipation, stable composition, no disqualifying spillover and **parallel trends in the untreated potential outcome**, this may identify a treated-group ATT of3. It is not enough that pre-period observed trajectories look similar. Multiple adoption dates with heterogeneous effects generally cannot be collapsed into one unqualified two-way fixed-effects causal coefficient.

**RD1 — A sharp cutoff provides an explicitly *local* contrast.** Assume treatment begins at running score **50**, with two fabricated observations just below cutoff **(48,5),(49,6)** and two at or above **(50,9),(51,10)**. The deterministic local linear extrapolation from below predicts left-side outcome at50 **7**, and right-side fitted outcome at50 **9**, giving an *algebraic jump* **2**. Four fitted points cannot independently justify continuity of potential outcomes, absence of threshold manipulation, bandwidth choice, sampling variance or the conclusion that the jump equals a causal effect for people away from cutoff. A fuzzy RD further requires a discontinuity in actual treatment uptake and its own identifying assumptions.

**IV1 — A Wald ratio targets a local complier effect only conditionally.** An invented binary instrument has **E[A|Z=1]=.6**, **E[A|Z=0]=.2**, so treatment first-stage difference **.4**. Mean outcome difference is **E[Y|Z=1]=1.8** versus **E[Y|Z=0]=1.0**, reduced-form difference **.8**. The ratio is **.8/.4=2** outcome units per induced treatment. Relevance, independence, no direct instrument→outcome effect (**exclusion**), monotonicity and correct treatment versions are additional substantive assumptions for interpreting this as a **local average treatment effect for compliers**; it is not a universal ATE or evidence that any conveniently correlated instrument is valid.

**M1 — Controlled direct versus total effect in an exact SCM.** Suppose a toy manipulable mediator satisfies **M(a)=a** and outcome obeys `Y(a,m)=a+2m`. Its *total* contrast between setting treatment1 and0 while each receives its natural mediator is **Y(1,M(1))−Y(0,M(0))=3−0=3**. For either fixed mediator value m, the *controlled direct* contrast is **Y(1,m)−Y(0,m)=1**. The difference2 is a model-specific mediated contribution and cannot be recovered by automatically controlling for a post-treatment mediator in an observational regression. Natural direct/indirect effect identification may require stronger cross-world assumptions and valid mediator interventions.

**LG1 — A finite dynamic treatment regime, not an RL reward oracle.** In a fabricated two-decision SCM, baseline action **A0∈{0,1}** causally changes subsequent binary covariate L1 with **P(L1=1|do(A0=0))=.3**, **P(L1=1|do(A0=1))=.6**. At the second decision apply dynamic treatment rule **A1=L1**; outcome is `Y=2A0+A1+L1`. Thus under initial action0, regime mean is **2×.3=.6**; under initial action1 it is **2+2×.6=3.2**; their causal regime difference in this **known stipulated SCM** is **2.6**. Estimating these from observational time-indexed records requires sequential exchangeability, valid treatment version/consistency and positive action support **at every relevant history**. Naive adjustment for post-A0 L1 can destroy part of the total effect, and no observed logged policy score grants permission for actual consequential intervention.

**H1 — Conditional effects and transported population means.** Two hypothetical pre-treatment strata have **conditional treatment risk-difference effects low +.20, high −.10**. In original target population with low/high weights **.75/.25**, weighted ATE = **.75×.20+.25×(−.10)=+.125**. In a different target population with weights **.25/.75**, weighted effect is **.25×.20+.75×(−.10)=−.025**, under the explicit **unverified assumption that conditional effects stay invariant across target settings** and relevant support exists. A treatment helping on average in one population can harm on average in another without arithmetic contradiction. Sparse subgroup estimates or personally inferred traits do not establish an individual treatment effect.

**Assumption failure and valid refusal.** A graph, fitted propensity, double-robust estimator, exact randomization p-value, trial emulation, DiD pretrend, IV first stage or model-based counterfactual may all be numerically well defined while a crucial causal assumption is false. Learners must identify exchangeability/positivity/consistency, interference, graph completeness, actual assignment, measurement timing, missingness, clustering, attrition, instrument exclusion, treatment leakage, transported support and unmeasured bias as appropriate. The correct assessment decision when an effect is not identified is **UNIDENTIFIED / HOLD / REVISE DESIGN**, not invented causal certainty. All exact figures are synthetic references; **actual learner inference and falsification require executed source-controlled simulations**, with observed result and uncertainty reported separately.
